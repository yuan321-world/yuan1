    科学BBC Future2026 年 3 月 14 日

    Why We Crave Sugar, and Why Willpower Is the Wrong Tool

    难度 B2词数 528生词率 4.2%预计 6 分钟

    Most people perceive a sugar craving as a failure of discipline. The physiology suggests something closer to the opposite. When blood glucose falls, the brain does not simply register hunger; it actively raises the value of sweet food, and it does so before you are aware of any decision being made.

    In one experiment, volunteers who slept less than six hours reported stronger cravings the following afternoon than those who slept eight. The gap was not small. The sleep-deprived group ate roughly 300 extra calories, and almost all of it came from snacks rather than meals. Nothing about their intentions had changed. Only their physiology had.

    The threshold at which a food starts to feel rewarding is not fixed. It shifts with sleep, with stress, and even with the time of day. That is why a habit which feels effortless on a rested Tuesday can collapse on a Thursday after a bad night. The habit did not change. The threshold did.

    There is a practical consequence here. If the craving is partly a matter of physiology, treating it as a purely moral problem is likely to fail, and the failure will feel personal when it is not. The volunteers were asked to plan a breakfast they would look forward to. Those who wrote down a specific, enjoyable meal reported less snacking later, which is a strange result if you believe the whole thing is about willpower.

    None of this means that discipline is useless. It means that discipline works better when it is aimed at the right target. Rearranging a kitchen is easier than resisting a kitchen at eleven at night, and the running total of small environmental changes eventually outweighs the one heroic decision you were planning to make on Monday.
